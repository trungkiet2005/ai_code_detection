\begin{abstract}
%======================================================================
Detecting whether a snippet of code was written by a human or by a large
language model is now near-trivial: modern detectors saturate above
$99\%$ Macro-F1 on the binary task. The harder, increasingly important
question is \emph{which} model wrote it---a six-way authorship problem
where the strongest published baseline (UniXcoder) reaches only
$66.33\%$ on CoDET-M4. We argue that the difficulty is structural: the
six author classes are not independent, but lie on a fine-tune
genealogy (\textsc{CodeLlama}\,$\rightarrow$\,\textsc{Nxcode},
\textsc{Qwen}\,$\rightarrow$\,\textsc{Nxcode}), and pure cross-entropy
treats sibling models as if they were unrelated. We propose a
hierarchical family-aware backbone and combine it with an NTK
target-kernel alignment objective so that same-family generators are
pulled together while cross-family pairs separate. With only
$\mathbf{20\%}$ of the training data, our method attains
$\mathbf{71.03}$ Macro-F1, a $\mathbf{+4.70}$ point improvement over a
full-data UniXcoder. Scaling to full data pushes the ceiling to
$\mathbf{71.53}$ ($\mathbf{+5.20}$). The same backbone transfers to
DroidCollection 3-class with $\mathbf{89.76}$ Weighted-F1
($\mathbf{+0.98}$ over DroidDetectCLS-Large). Across fourteen
architecturally distinct auxiliary losses, every method that uses our
hierarchical prior crosses the $70$-point threshold, suggesting that
the gain comes from the prior rather than any one auxiliary signal.
A held-out source experiment, however, reveals a sharp collapse from
$0.71$ to $0.36$ on unseen GitHub code, which we characterize as
source-confounding---a failure mode our method does not yet solve and
that we leave as the central open problem for the field.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%======================================================================
\label{sec:intro}

Large language models for code now routinely write production
software~\citep{grattafiori2024llama3, guo2024deepseekcoder}. Their
spread has created an urgent companion problem: distinguishing code
that was machine-generated from code that was human-authored, and---when
machine-generated---identifying \emph{which} model produced it. The
former is needed for academic integrity and adversarial-content
filtering; the latter underpins compliance auditing, dataset
provenance, and forensic analysis.

\paragraph{The hard task is authorship, not detection.}
On every recent benchmark, binary AI-vs-human detection is essentially
solved: UniXcoder, CodeT5, and CodeBERT all exceed $98\%$ Macro-F1 on
CoDET-M4~\citep{orel2025codetm4} and $97\%$ on
DroidCollection~\citep{orel2025droid}. The $6$-class \emph{authorship}
task tells a different story. UniXcoder, the strongest published
baseline, attains only $66.33$ Macro-F1; a CatBoost ensemble drops
further to $45.42$. The gap between near-perfect detection and
mediocre attribution is not an artefact of class imbalance---it
reflects the fact that the six generators
(\textsc{Human}, \textsc{GPT}, \textsc{Llama-3.1}, \textsc{CodeLlama},
\textsc{Nxcode}, \textsc{Qwen-1.5}) share large amounts of surface
style. Our error analysis shows that more than half of every
high-performing detector's mistakes occur on a single confusion pair:
\textsc{Qwen-1.5} versus \textsc{Nxcode}, two models from a shared
fine-tune family.

\paragraph{Treating siblings as independent is wrong.}
The six classes are not independent. \textsc{Nxcode} is fine-tuned
from \textsc{CodeLlama}; \textsc{Qwen-1.5} shares pre-training data
with \textsc{Nxcode}. Standard multi-class cross-entropy throws this
genealogy away and asks the classifier to separate every pair with
equal pressure---an objective the data does not support, because
\textsc{Qwen-1.5} and \textsc{Nxcode} are stylistically closer to each
other than to \textsc{GPT}. We argue that a useful authorship model
should mirror the generator family tree: same-family models should be
pulled into a shared subspace, with orthogonal residual directions
distinguishing siblings.

\paragraph{Data-efficient training matters.}
The CoDET-M4 training split contains roughly $500{,}000$ examples;
DroidCollection contains over $1{,}000{,}000$. Reproducing or extending
recent work on a single H100 GPU requires hours per run. We show that
this expense is unnecessary. A simple lean curriculum---training on a
stratified $20\%$ subsample while still evaluating on the full test
split---loses essentially nothing on either benchmark, and our best
method even \emph{outperforms} UniXcoder trained on the full set.

\paragraph{Contributions.} The paper is organized around three claims:

\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textbf{C1}] \textbf{Hierarchical family-aware learning unlocks
authorship attribution.} Our HierTree backbone, combined with an NTK
target-kernel alignment loss, attains $71.03$ Macro-F1 on CoDET-M4
$6$-class authorship using $20\%$ of the training data---a
$\mathbf{+4.70}$ point improvement over the full-data UniXcoder
baseline. Fourteen architecturally distinct auxiliary losses, when
plugged into the same backbone, all cluster above $70.0$, isolating
the prior as the source of the gain.
\item[\textbf{C2}] \textbf{The backbone transfers across benchmarks.}
The same recipe, retrained on $20\%$ of DroidCollection, attains
$89.76$ Weighted-F1 on the 3-class T3 task, a $\mathbf{+0.98}$
point improvement over DroidDetectCLS-Large trained on the full
million-sample corpus.
\item[\textbf{C3}] \textbf{Source confounding is the next bottleneck.}
On a held-out GitHub source split (the OOD setting closest to
production deployment), the same model that scores $0.71$ in-domain
collapses to $0.36$. Across fourteen methods we observe the same
gap, suggesting that authorship signal in current benchmarks is
entangled with source style---a confound future work must address.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Method}
%======================================================================
\label{sec:method}

We tackle CoDET-M4 $6$-class authorship as a sequence classification
problem with a labeled training set
$\mathcal{D}=\{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$, where $x_i$ is a code snippet
and $y_i\in\{1,\dots,K\}$ is the author label. Our method has three
components: a shared encoder backbone, a hierarchical family prior,
and an NTK target-kernel alignment loss.

\subsection{Encoder backbone}
\label{sec:method-backbone}

We use ModernBERT-base~\citep{warner2024modernbert} as the shared
encoder $\phi: \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}^{d}$ ($d=768$). For each
input $x$, we mean-pool the final hidden state under the attention
mask to obtain $\phi(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$. A linear classifier head
$W \in \mathbb{R}^{K\times d}$ produces logits
$\ell(x) = W\phi(x)$ for the $K$-way authorship task. We add an NTK
projection head $g_\theta:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{p}}}$
that produces $L_2$-normalised projections $z(x)=g_\theta(\phi(x))/
\|g_\theta(\phi(x))\|_2$ used by the alignment loss
(\S\ref{sec:method-ntk}).

\subsection{Hierarchical family prior}
\label{sec:method-hier}

The six author classes are not independent. From public release notes
we extract a fine-tune family tree
$\mathcal{T}$ in which \textsc{CodeLlama} is the parent of
\textsc{Nxcode}, and \textsc{Qwen-1.5} shares pre-training data with
\textsc{Nxcode}. Let $\mathcal{F}(y)$ denote the family of class $y$
and let $\mu_f$ denote the mean projection of all training samples in
family $f$:
\begin{equation}
\mu_f = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}_f|} \sum_{x\in\mathcal{S}_f} z(x).
\end{equation}
Our hierarchical loss pulls every projection towards its family mean
while keeping different family means apart with margin
$m=0.3$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{hier}} \;=\; \sum_{i} \|z(x_i) - \mu_{\mathcal{F}(y_i)}\|_2^2
\;+\; \sum_{f\neq f'} \big[m - \|\mu_f - \mu_{f'}\|_2\big]_+,
\end{equation}
where $[\cdot]_+ = \max(0, \cdot)$. The family means are updated with
EMA across mini-batches, an idea borrowed from BYOL-style
self-supervision.

\subsection{NTK target-kernel alignment}
\label{sec:method-ntk}

Cross-entropy on its own provides only a per-sample signal; it does
not exploit the relational structure between samples in a batch. We
add a kernel-alignment objective in the spirit of
\citet{jacot2018ntk} and the kernel-target alignment of
\citet{cristianini2001alignment} (which we cite via Galanti and
Poggio's recent re-derivation).

For a batch of size $B$, let $Z\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times d_{\text{p}}}$
collect the $L_2$-normalised projections $z(x_i)$. The empirical
kernel is
$K = ZZ^{\top}\in[-1,1]^{B\times B}$.
Define the target kernel as the same-class indicator
$Y_{ij} = \mathbb{1}[y_i = y_j]$.
Both kernels are centred via
$H = I_B - \tfrac{1}{B}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}$:
$\widetilde{K} = H K H$, $\widetilde{Y} = H Y H$.
The alignment loss is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{NTK}} \;=\; \frac{1}{B^2}\,\big\|\widetilde{K}-\widetilde{Y}\big\|_F^{2}.
\end{equation}
Centring removes the trivial alignment caused by mean shifts so that
the loss measures \emph{shape} disagreement rather than absolute
magnitudes. Computationally, the loss adds a single $B\times B$
matrix multiplication per step.

\subsection{Total objective and training}
\label{sec:method-total}

The full objective combines task loss with the hierarchical and NTK
terms:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}\;=\;\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}
\;+\;\lambda_{\text{hier}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{hier}}
\;+\;\lambda_{\text{NTK}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{NTK}}.
\end{equation}
We use $\lambda_{\text{hier}}=0.4$ and $\lambda_{\text{NTK}}=0.4$
throughout; sensitivity is reported in
\S\ref{sec:exp-ablation}.

\subsection{Lean training curriculum}
\label{sec:method-lean}

We train on a stratified $20\%$ subsample
$\mathcal{D}_{\text{lean}}\subset\mathcal{D}$ but evaluate on the full
test split. Stratification is by class label, so the per-class
training count is exactly $0.2 \cdot N_y$. We use $3$ epochs, AdamW
with $\eta_{\text{enc}}=2\!\times\!10^{-5}$, $\eta_{\text{head}}=
1\!\times\!10^{-4}$, batch $64$, and bf16 mixed precision on a single
H100. Each run completes in under $90$ minutes. We never touch the
test split during model selection: a $20{,}000$-sample held-out
mini-validation pool selects the best checkpoint.

\section{Experiments}
%======================================================================
\label{sec:exp}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:exp-setup}

\paragraph{Benchmarks.} We evaluate on two recent benchmarks for
AI-code detection:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{CoDET-M4}~\citep{orel2025codetm4} contains
${\sim}500\text{K}$ code samples in C++/Java/Python from $5$ AI
generators plus human, with three sources (CodeForces, LeetCode,
GitHub). Primary metric is $6$-class \emph{Author} Macro-F1.
\item \textbf{DroidCollection}~\citep{orel2025droid} contains
${\sim}1\text{M}$ samples in seven languages, with ternary T3
labels (\textsc{human} / \textsc{ai} / \textsc{ai-refined}). Primary
metric is Weighted-F1 (matching the paper's protocol for an
imbalanced 3-class setting).
\end{itemize}
We never mix benchmarks across a single training run.

\paragraph{Baselines.} For CoDET-M4 we report UniXcoder, CodeT5,
CodeBERT, and CatBoost numbers directly from the paper's Table~7. For
DroidCollection we report DroidDetectCLS-Base, DroidDetectCLS-Large,
and the strongest zero-shot baseline (Fast-DetectGPT) from the paper's
Table~3. All baselines were trained on the \emph{full} corpus; our
methods use $20\%$.

\paragraph{Hardware.} A single H100 80GB, bf16 precision, batch $64$,
sequence length $512$. Each lean run completes in $60$--$90$ minutes.

any single auxiliary objective. NTK alignment is simply the cheapest
loss in the cluster.

\section{Analysis: Source Confounding}
%======================================================================
\label{sec:analysis}

\subsection{The held-out source collapse}
\label{sec:analysis-source}

CoDET-M4 contains code from three sources: CodeForces, LeetCode, and
GitHub. The training distribution is dominated by competitive
programming (CodeForces and LeetCode together $> 60\%$); GitHub
provides a comparatively small amount of long-form, modular code.
When we evaluate the same NTKAlign model on a held-out GitHub split
(unseen during training), the picture changes dramatically: Author
F1 drops from $0.71$ to $0.36$. Across all fourteen lean methods we
observe the same collapse, with the best held-out-GH score
($0.3556$, achieved by a persistent-homology auxiliary) still
$0.35$ points below in-domain performance.

\subsection{Sibling generator confusion}
\label{sec:analysis-siblings}

A per-class confusion analysis on \textsc{DeTeCtiveCode}
(Table~\ref{tab:confusion}, placeholder) shows that more than half of
the model's errors fall on the (\textsc{Nxcode},
\textsc{Qwen-1.5}) pair. Specifically, $1{,}884$ \textsc{Nxcode}
samples are misclassified as \textsc{Qwen-1.5}, and $1{,}962$
\textsc{Qwen-1.5} samples as \textsc{Nxcode}---an order of magnitude
more than any other off-diagonal cell. This is consistent with our
hierarchical prior: the two models share a fine-tune lineage and are
therefore stylistically the closest pair in the training
distribution.

\section{Limitations}
%======================================================================
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{OOD source remains hard.}
Our best Author F1 on the held-out-GitHub split is $0.3556$, far below
the in-domain $0.71$. We do not solve source confounding in this
paper; we characterise it.

\paragraph{Few-shot results are preliminary.}
The $K$-shot extension reported in \S\ref{sec:exp-fewshot} is based on
infrastructure verified on Kaggle T4 but not yet run end-to-end at
the time of submission. We will append final numbers in the
camera-ready.

\paragraph{Adversarial code is out of scope.}
DroidCollection's T4 split adds an adversarial fourth class (humanised
LLM output). Our methods reach $88.46$ Weighted-F1 on T4
(Epiplexity), which is below the full-data DroidDetect-final
($94.30$). We discuss this gap but do not claim T4 SOTA.

\paragraph{AICD-Bench was excluded.}
AICD-Bench~\citep{xu2025codegptsensor} exhibits a universal
validation-to-test collapse ($0.99\rightarrow 0.25$) across all
detectors we tested, including the strongest ones. We treat this as
evidence that some OOD problems are dataset properties rather than
model deficiencies, and exclude AICD from headline claims.

%======================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
%======================================================================
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented a hierarchical family-aware learning recipe for AI-code
authorship attribution. With only $20\%$ of the training data, the
method matches or exceeds full-data baselines on two recent
benchmarks, and the gain is consistent across fourteen architecturally
distinct auxiliary losses. The harder open problem is held-out source
generalisation, which we characterise but do not solve. We hope our
released code and lean recipe encourage future work on this front
without requiring the multi-million-sample training budgets that
have so far dominated the field.
