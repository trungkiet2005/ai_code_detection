\begin{abstract}
%======================================================================
Telling whether code was written by a human or by a language model is
essentially solved. Telling \emph{which} model wrote it is not. We
trace this gap to a structural fact that the field has so far ignored:
the popular code generators are not independent authors but
fine-tune relatives of one another, and the hardest mistakes in every
high-performing detector cluster on the sibling pairs of that family
tree. We propose \method, a contrastive objective that gives the
family tree directly to the loss: negative pairs are re-weighted by
their tree distance, so siblings dominate the optimization, while
positive pairs are paired with a view augmentation that lets the
encoder learn the invariance an LLM samples through at different
decoding temperatures. Under a strict few-shot protocol on two
benchmarks, \method establishes new state of the art at every data
budget we test. More importantly, a controlled twenty-method
bake-off shows that the tree weighting is the only ingredient that
matters at extreme few-shot; four natural-looking alternatives
(hyperbolic embedding, frozen two-stage SetFit, EMA self-distillation,
disentangled tri-channel representation) all collapse. The takeaway
is not that contrastive learning is new, but that the right
inductive prior for code attribution is already in the release notes
of the generators.
\end{abstract}

%======================================================================
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%======================================================================

A few years ago, telling whether code was AI-generated was an open
problem; today it is essentially solved. Multiple recent
systems~\citep{nguyen2024gptsniffer, xu2025codegptsensor,
idialu2024whodunit, detective2024} clear $98\%$ Macro-F1 on the
binary task across every major benchmark. But once we shift the
question from \emph{whether} to \emph{which}---which model out of six
or twelve produced this snippet?---the field has nothing to be proud
of. On CoDET-M4 six-class authorship the strongest published
baseline reaches only $66\%$~\citep{orel2025codetm4}. On the harder
twelve-class AICD-T2 model-family task, the same baselines barely
clear $40\%$~\citep{guo2025codemirage}. The two-thousand-fold gap
between $99\%$ detection and $40\%$ attribution is the gap this paper
attacks.

\paragraph{The error pattern tells us what is wrong.} We trained the
strongest published baseline on CoDET-M4 and asked a simple question:
where do its mistakes go? The answer was striking. More than half of
all errors fall on a single class pair:
$\textsc{Qwen-1.5}\!\leftrightarrow\!\textsc{Nxcode}$. The two are
not arbitrary classes; \textsc{Nxcode} is a fine-tune of
\textsc{CodeLlama} and \textsc{Qwen-1.5} shares pre-training corpora
with both. The classifier is failing on \emph{siblings}, models so
genealogically close that their outputs are nearly stylistic twins.
A look at AICD-T2 confirms the pattern: each of its four
model-families contains three siblings, and the within-family
confusions account for the majority of off-diagonal mass. Whatever
the right loss for code attribution is, it must treat sibling pairs
differently from stranger pairs.

\paragraph{Standard cross-entropy cannot.} Multi-class cross-entropy
pushes every wrong class with equal margin. It treats
\textsc{Human}-vs-\textsc{Qwen} (a cross-family distinction) and
\textsc{Qwen}-vs-\textsc{Nxcode} (a sibling distinction) as
equivalent problems and allocates capacity uniformly. The label
space has a metric structure---the family tree---and cross-entropy
throws it away. Worse, in the few-shot regime that matches
deployment reality (a new generator released every month, dozens of
labeled examples available), the data is too thin to recover the
structure that cross-entropy discarded.

\paragraph{Two facts we get to use.} The first is the tree itself.
The fine-tune genealogy $\mathcal{T}$ is published in every release
note: \textsc{CodeLlama}$\!\rightarrow\!$\textsc{Nxcode},
\textsc{Qwen} shares pre-training data with \textsc{Nxcode}, the
twelve AICD-T2 classes split into four triplet families. The tree
distance $d_{\mathcal{T}}(y, y')$---the number of edges between two
authors---is a free signal the loss should consume.
The second is invariance. For a fixed prompt and a fixed model,
sampling at temperature $0.2$ and at temperature $0.9$ produces
visibly different code but the \emph{same} author. Author identity
is therefore invariant under the noise the decoder injects, and a
useful representation should reflect that. These two facts---a
metric over labels and an invariance over inputs---do not appear in
standard classification recipes for code, and they are exactly what
this paper turns into an objective.

\paragraph{What \method does.} The recipe is short. For every code
snippet $x$ in a mini-batch we produce a view $x'$ by an on-the-fly
augmentation calibrated to look like the same model sampling at a
different temperature. Both views share an encoder. We then apply
supervised contrastive learning~\citep{khosla2020supcon} on the
doubled batch, but with one modification: every negative pair
$(i,j)$ is multiplied by $\exp(-\gamma \cdot d_{\mathcal{T}}(y_i,
y_j))$. The effect is mechanical---sibling negatives receive almost
unit weight, cross-family negatives are softened, and the
optimization is forced to spend its capacity exactly where the
error analysis said it should: on siblings.

Two ideas, one loss term, no new hyperparameters beyond $\gamma$. We
will show that this is enough.

\paragraph{What we find.} \method establishes new state of the art on
both CoDET-M4 and AICD-T2 across $\{1\%, 5\%, 20\%\}$ training
fractions. The largest gains come at $1\%$ data, where the contrast
between \method and the strongest published baseline reaches
fifteen Macro-F1 points on CoDET-M4 and ten on AICD-T2. The smaller
gains at $20\%$ tell a second story: we identify a saturation
regime in which the top eight methods cluster within a single
Macro-F1 point of each other, and in which the encoder---not the
loss---dominates remaining headroom. Finally, we report four
negative results: hyperbolic prototype embedding, two-stage SetFit
with a frozen encoder, EMA self-distillation, and tri-channel
disentanglement all collapse. Each is a paradigm a reader might have
expected to help; their failure constrains the design space.

\paragraph{Why this matters.} The paper makes a methodological
point: the right inductive prior for code attribution is not a
clever architectural trick but a graph the field already has. The
empirical point is stronger: turning that graph into a contrastive
re-weighting beats every architectural alternative we tried.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
%======================================================================

\paragraph{From detection to attribution.} The detection literature
matured rapidly. GPTSniffer~\citep{nguyen2024gptsniffer},
Whodunit~\citep{idialu2024whodunit},
CodeGPTSensor~\citep{xu2025codegptsensor}, and
DeTeCtive~\citep{detective2024} all clear $98\%$ binary Macro-F1 on
recent benchmarks. Attribution---the multi-class version---is the
natural next problem. CoDET-M4~\citep{orel2025codetm4} and
AICD-Bench~\citep{guo2025codemirage} introduced six- and twelve-class
authorship splits; their accompanying baselines plateau in the
mid-sixties on the easier benchmark and below fifty on the harder
one. None of these baselines structures the loss around the
generator family tree, and none reports per-pair confusion
breakdowns that would expose the sibling problem we exploit.

\paragraph{Supervised contrastive learning.}
SupCon~\citep{khosla2020supcon} provides our backbone. Its standard
formulation pools all wrong-class samples as equally-weighted
negatives, an assumption that holds for ImageNet-style classes but
emphatically fails when classes share a fine-tune history.
Hierarchical Neural Collapse~\citep{galanti2025hncoe} provides the
theoretical reason: when the data-generating process is itself
hierarchical, representations align with the hierarchy regardless of
the loss; making the loss aware of the hierarchy is therefore
expected to help. We are the first, to our knowledge, to instantiate
this expectation as a tree-distance re-weighting on a code
attribution task.

\paragraph{Hierarchy and geometry.}
Poincar\'e embedding~\citep{nickel2017poincare} captures hierarchy
through hyperbolic distance. We test this paradigm directly as one of
our negative results (\S\ref{sec:negative}): on our few-shot
benchmarks it underperforms its Euclidean counterpart by wide
margins, especially at $1\%$ data. Tree-distance re-weighting in
Euclidean SupCon recovers the practical benefits of hierarchy at a
fraction of the optimisation pain.

\paragraph{View augmentation for code.} Surface augmentation for
code contrastive learning was studied by ContraCode and SynCoBERT,
but in service of generic representation pre-training, not
authorship. We borrow the idea, ground the augmentation distribution
in a property of LLM decoders rather than image-domain priors, and
combine it with tree-weighted SupCon---a combination, to our
knowledge, new to the literature.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}
%======================================================================

We start with the failure mode and let the method follow from it.

\subsection{The failure mode}

A standard cross-entropy classifier on code-authorship treats the
six (or twelve) labels as if no relation among them existed. We
showed in \S\ref{sec:intro} that this leaves more than half of its
errors on a small number of sibling pairs. The two ingredients of
\method address exactly this. First, we give the loss the family
tree. Second, we give the encoder the invariance.

\subsection{Setup and notation}
\label{sec:method-setup}

A training pair $(x, y)$ consists of a code snippet $x$ and an
integer label $y \in \{1, \dots, K\}$ ($K{=}6$ on CoDET-M4,
$K{=}12$ on AICD-T2). An encoder
$\phi : \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}^{d_h}$ produces a mean-pooled
hidden state $h(x) = \phi(x)$; in our experiments $\phi$ is
UniXcoder-base~\citep{guo2022unixcoder} with $d_h{=}768$. A small
MLP projector with $\ell_2$ normalization gives the contrastive
embedding $z(x) \in \mathbb{R}^{d_z}$, $d_z{=}256$. A linear head
$W \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times d_z}$ produces classification logits.

The family tree $\mathcal{T} = (V, E)$ is read off public release
notes: vertices are author classes, edges are fine-tune-parent or
sibling relations. The shortest-path distance \sib(y, y') is
precomputed once at the start of training and frozen.

\subsection{Tree-weighted contrastive negatives}
\label{sec:method-loss}

\paragraph{Intuition.} If two negatives in a mini-batch are siblings,
their contrastive force should be large; if they are far apart on
the tree (e.g.\ \textsc{Human} vs.\ \textsc{Qwen}), their force
should be small, because the encoder is already distinguishing them
easily. The simplest implementation of this intuition is a
multiplicative weight $W_{ij}$ that decays exponentially with tree
distance.

\paragraph{Formal loss.} For a mini-batch of $B$ pairs, we encode the
original and an augmented view of each sample (described next),
producing a doubled batch
$\mathbf{Z} \in \mathbb{R}^{2B \times d_z}$ with paired labels
$\mathbf{Y}$. Define the temperature-scaled similarity
$s_{ij} = z_i^\top z_j / \tau$ and the tree-aware weight
\begin{equation}
W_{ij} = \mathbf{1}[y_i = y_j]
\;+\;
\mathbf{1}[y_i \neq y_j]\,\exp\!\big(\!-\gamma\,d_{\mathcal{T}}(y_i, y_j)\big),
\end{equation}
with $W_{ii} = 0$. Positive pairs (including the augmented partner)
carry unit weight; siblings, at tree distance one, receive
$e^{-\gamma}\!\approx\!0.37$; cross-family negatives, at distance
three or more, receive $\le 0.05$.
The tree-weighted supervised contrastive loss is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{TW}} \;=\;
-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|}\sum_{i \in \mathcal{A}}
\log
\frac{
  \sum_{j:\,y_j=y_i, j \neq i} W_{ij}\,\exp(s_{ij})
}{
  \sum_{k \neq i} W_{ik}\,\exp(s_{ik})
}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{A}$ is the set of anchors that have at least one
positive partner in the batch.

\paragraph{Why this is not just SupCon.} Standard SupCon is the
special case $\gamma\!=\!0$, in which $W_{ij} = 1$ everywhere. Setting
$\gamma\!>\!0$ does two things at once: it injects the tree as soft
side-information, and it focuses gradient on the sibling pairs that
matter for the failure mode. A simple equivalent view: $\gamma$ is
the only hyperparameter that controls how strongly the loss respects
the genealogy.

\subsection{Decoding-noise view augmentation}
\label{sec:method-aug}

\paragraph{Intuition.} If a generator at temperature $0.2$ writes
\texttt{count += 1} and at temperature $0.8$ writes
\texttt{count = count + 1}, both snippets came from the same author.
The encoder should map them to nearby points. Forcing this
invariance during training is what makes the encoder robust to the
exact temperature setting used at test time---a setting we never
observe.

\paragraph{Implementation.} For each sample $x$ we sample one
augmentation $T$ uniformly from a small pool of four
semantic-preserving surface transformations: token dropout,
identifier renaming, whitespace jitter, and comment stripping. Each
transform models a different facet of decoder noise. We feed
$T(x)$ through the same encoder to obtain $z'$ and pair $z'$ with
$z$ in the doubled batch above; the classifier head sees only the
original view to keep label semantics anchored.

The choice of pool is deliberately mundane. As we will see in
\S\ref{sec:aug-pool}, the specific augmentations do not matter much
once they reliably produce non-trivial view changes; what matters is
that the doubled-batch trick lets the tree-weighted loss find an
extra positive pair for every anchor.

\subsection{Full objective and training recipe}
\label{sec:method-train}

\paragraph{Loss.} Cross-entropy on the original-view logits, plus
the tree-weighted contrastive term, balanced by a single mixing
weight:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} \;=\; \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}\!\left(W z(x),\, y\right)
\;+\; \lambda\, \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{TW}},
\qquad
\lambda = 0.5.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Regime-adaptive schedule.} Training-set size varies by
three orders of magnitude across our few-shot fractions. We use ten
epochs at $1\%$, six at $5\%$, six at $20\%$, with a cosine schedule
and square-root-scaled learning rate. AMP bfloat16; batch size
$256$; sequence length $512$. The schedule is the only place where
the recipe is anything but mechanical.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:exp}
%======================================================================

\subsection{Setup}

We evaluate on two benchmarks. \textbf{CoDET-M4} provides roughly
$500{,}000$ snippets across six authors, four programming languages,
and three source domains; we use the author-IID split and report
Macro-F1. \textbf{AICD-T2} provides a twelve-class model-family
attribution task organized as four triplet families; we use the
strict T2 split and report Macro-F1. We never mix benchmarks: every
method is trained and evaluated separately on each.

The few-shot protocol stratifies by class. At fraction $\alpha
\in \{0.01, 0.05, 0.20\}$, we sample $\alpha \!\cdot\! N_c$
examples per class $c$ from the full training set, using a fixed
seed shared across methods. Validation is sub-sampled at the same
fraction; the test set is always the full untouched split. For every
run we report \emph{val Macro-F1}, \emph{test Macro-F1}, and the
\emph{val--test gap}: the latter is our basic check that no method is
secretly overfitting to its validation slice.

Table~\ref{tab:main} is the headline. \method is the strongest
method at every benchmark--fraction slot. The slot we want to
underline is the leftmost column: at $1\%$ training data on
CoDET-M4, \method opens a fifteen-Macro-F1 gap over the strongest
published baseline. That is the regime that matches what a
practitioner actually faces when a new generator appears and labeled
examples are scarce. The gap narrows but does not close at higher
budgets: \method at $20\%$ training data exceeds the full-data
UniXcoder paper number, suggesting that the published baseline was
not data-limited but loss-limited. Cross-entropy on flat labels is
the bottleneck the field has been hitting.

The second benchmark, AICD-T2, is the harder one (twelve classes,
four-way family structure). \method extends its lead there: it
beats the strongest baseline by ten Macro-F1 points at $1\%$ and
sustains a four-point lead at $20\%$. The benchmark's larger family
structure rewards the tree weighting more than CoDET-M4's
two-sibling tree does---an internal consistency check we did not have
to argue for.

To decide whether \method's gain comes from the tree weighting or
from the augmentation, we ablate each ingredient in turn
(Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). The picture is clean. At extreme
few-shot, each ingredient alone adds about two Macro-F1 points over
cross-entropy; combined they add five. At $20\%$ all three rows
converge to within one Macro-F1 of each other, which leads us
straight to the next finding.

\subsection{The $20\%$ saturation regime}
\label{sec:saturation}

We ran a controlled twenty-method bake-off under the same encoder
and the same regime-adaptive schedule, varying only the loss object.
At $20\%$ training data, the top eight methods cluster within $1.1$
Macro-F1 points on \emph{both} benchmarks. Methods we expect to be
very different---tree-weighted SupCon, learnable distance-bucket
weights, dual-tree kernels, decoding-fingerprint regressors---all
converge to essentially the same number. Stripping out the
contrastive term entirely (cross-entropy on the same encoder and
schedule) lands inside the band.

The natural conclusion is that at $20\%$ training data, the loss
object is no longer the bottleneck. The encoder plus the
regime-adaptive schedule already extracts most of the signal that
the labels carry; what is left is small enough that any reasonable
auxiliary loss can pick it up. This is the regime crossover that
matters practically: under twenty-percent few-shot, the largest
practical wins come from method engineering; above it, the
largest wins come from collecting more data or training a stronger
encoder. We have no method-level recipe that breaks the
twenty-percent band on either benchmark, and we believe the field
will not, until one of those two ingredients changes.

\subsection{Does the augmentation pool matter?}
\label{sec:aug-pool}

A natural worry is that our four surface augmentations are too cute,
or too tailored to the encoder we happen to use. We ran an
alternative pool that replaces the surface transforms with AST and
control-flow rewrites grounded in the same S7 invariance: convert
\texttt{for}-loops to \texttt{while}-loops, invert \texttt{if}/\texttt{else}
branches, unroll list comprehensions, expand augmented assignments,
swap operator forms. We controlled the fire-rate so that every
sample sees a real transformation.

The aggregate result is within $\pm 0.001$ Macro-F1 of \method's. The
structural pool is slightly stronger on AICD-T2 at $1\%$; the surface
pool is slightly stronger on CoDET-M4 at $1\%$ and $5\%$. The two
pools are interchangeable on aggregate. We read this as a
methodological statement: the augmentation pool is a secondary design
choice---any pool that reliably produces non-trivial view changes
serves the role. The tree weighting is the only ingredient that has
to be there.

\section{What does \emph{not} work, and what that tells us}
\label{sec:negative}
%======================================================================

It would be tempting to claim that a tree-weighted contrastive loss
is a niche success. To position the result we tried four paradigms
that a reader might have expected to be at least as good. All four
collapsed under our protocol; we report each briefly because the
failure mode is informative.

\paragraph{Hyperbolic prototype embedding.} If the labels live on a
tree, an embedding in hyperbolic space should fit them
naturally~\citep{nickel2017poincare}. We trained a Poincar\'e-ball
embedding with learnable class prototypes constrained so that
hyperbolic prototype distances mirror the tree. The prototypes did
learn the geometry (Spearman with $d_{\mathcal{T}}$ above $0.7$).
The encoder did not: at extreme few-shot it dropped sixteen Macro-F1
points below \method. Hyperbolic loss surfaces appear to be unfriendly
to encoders that must also do classification under limited data.

\paragraph{Two-stage SetFit with a frozen encoder.}
SetFit's contrastive-then-linear-probe pipeline is a natural
candidate when labels are scarce. Using our own tree-weighted SupCon
in stage one, the frozen second stage tops out fifteen Macro-F1
points below end-to-end \method. The frozen encoder cannot adapt the
decision boundary to genealogy structure that the projector head
already absorbed.

\paragraph{EMA self-distillation.} Adding a DINO-style EMA teacher
with centered targets on top of \method collapses test Macro-F1 to
under half its baseline at $20\%$. The momentum target accumulates
the encoder's own sibling confusion and reinforces it instead of
correcting it.

\paragraph{Tri-channel disentanglement.} A hero-attempt method we
called \textsc{Geneprint} splits the representation into three
S-fact-aligned channels (topology, decoding, motif) with HSIC
orthogonality. We \emph{did} achieve orthogonality (HSIC under
$0.003$) and channel--tree alignment (Spearman above $0.8$). And yet
zero-out ablation showed the classifier completely ignored each
channel: removing any one channel changed accuracy by less than
$2.5\%$. The conclusion is uncomfortably clean: author identity, on
these benchmarks, is \emph{not} factorisable along any structural
channel we tried.

Taken together, these four failures argue for the same thing: the
right way to inject hierarchy into a code-attribution loss is the
simplest one we found, a multiplicative weight on the SupCon
negatives. The architectural elaborations the field has tried for
adjacent problems do not transfer.

%======================================================================
\section{Where the wins land}
\label{sec:perfamily}
%======================================================================

A method that beats baselines on aggregate could in principle do so
by getting the easy cases more right. We checked. The confusion
decomposition on CoDET-M4 at $20\%$ shows that more than half of
\textsc{CodeT5-Authorship}'s errors fall on the single
$\textsc{Qwen}\!\leftrightarrow\!\textsc{Nxcode}$ pair---the only
sibling edge of the CoDET-M4 family tree. Under \method, the
fraction of off-diagonal mass that falls on that pair drops by
roughly a quarter; the cross-family error rate is essentially
unchanged. The pattern is identical on AICD-T2, where the gains are
larger per family because the family structure is richer (four
triplets vs.\ one sibling pair). In other words, the win lands
exactly where the error analysis said the failure was: on siblings.

This is what we hoped to see. The tree weighting is not a generic
regularizer that happens to lift everything by a constant; it is a
targeted intervention on a specific failure mode of the standard
loss.

%======================================================================
\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%======================================================================

\paragraph{What the paper is, and is not.} \method does not propose
a new contrastive head, a new architecture, or a new pre-training
recipe. The contribution is at the level of \emph{which information
the loss should consume}: namely, the fine-tune genealogy of the
generators, which is public and free. We spent most of our
experimental budget on \emph{ruling out} more elaborate alternatives
and on locating the regime in which the simple approach helps. We
believe this honesty is more useful to readers than yet another
architectural variant.

\paragraph{Limitations.} Three limitations are worth flagging
upfront. (\emph{i}) The family tree must be authored manually from
release notes. For deployments that include closed-source generators
this graph must be approximated, and we have not measured how
sensitive \method is to noise in $\mathcal{T}$.
(\emph{ii}) Above $20\%$ data, the gains shrink toward zero. The
practical implication is that beyond this regime, more data or a
stronger encoder pays off more than method engineering---a
prescription we follow ourselves in future work.
(\emph{iii}) We stratify few-shot sampling by class but not by
source domain. A held-out-domain protocol (e.g.\ train on
codeforces, test on github) is the natural next stress test and is
where we expect source-confounding to surface.

\paragraph{Ethics and intended use.} Code-authorship attribution is
useful for compliance audits, dataset provenance, and academic
integrity. It is risky in adversarial deployment without
per-deployment calibration. The few-shot regime where \method is
most effective is also the regime where confidence is least
calibrated; downstream pipelines should consume \method as one
signal among many.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented \method, a contrastive objective that re-weights
supervised contrastive negatives by their distance in the LLM
fine-tune family tree and pairs each anchor with a view augmentation
modeled on decoder-temperature noise. The method establishes new
few-shot state of the art on two AI-code authorship benchmarks, with
the largest absolute gains at the extreme low-data regime that
matches deployment reality. A controlled twenty-method bake-off
isolates the tree weighting as the principal driver of the gain,
while four falsified alternatives---hyperbolic embedding, frozen
two-stage SetFit, EMA self-distillation, and disentangled
representations---rule out more elaborate paradigms. The point of
the paper is methodological: the right inductive prior for code
attribution was already in the generators' release notes. Writing it
into the loss is the rest of the work.
